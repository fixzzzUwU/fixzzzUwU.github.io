Best Robo-Advisors for Beginners: How to Start Investing With $100

Most investing advice assumes you already have thousands of dollars sitting around. That's not most people's reality. If you're 22 to 40, paying rent, and trying to build a savings cushion, a hundred dollars might be all you can spare — and it's easy to assume that's not enough to matter.

It is enough. The hardest part of investing isn't the amount, it's getting started at all. Robo-advisors and micro-investing apps were built specifically for this situation: low or no minimums, automatic deposits, and portfolios managed for you. This guide compares the best robo-advisors for beginners and shows you exactly how to start investing with $100.

Why $100 Is a Realistic Starting Point

Two things changed the math for small investors. First, fractional shares: you can now own a slice of an index fund or stock instead of buying a full share. Second, robo-advisors automated the boring parts — rebalancing, dividend reinvestment, diversification — so you don't need to know how to pick stocks.

The bigger point is habit. A $100 starting balance plus a $50 monthly contribution teaches you the mechanics of investing while the stakes are small. You'll learn how deposits, allocations, and market swings actually feel before you're managing a larger sum.

What Actually Matters When Choosing a Beginner App

Minimums and fees

Look at two numbers: how much you need to open an account, and what you pay to keep it. Robo-advisors typically charge an annual management fee (often around 0.25% of your balance), while micro-investing apps favor flat monthly subscriptions. On a $100 balance, a $3 monthly fee is a huge percentage — so if your balance is tiny, a percentage-based advisor is usually cheaper than a subscription app.

Automation and portfolio quality

The best app is the one that runs without you thinking about it. Recurring transfers, automatic rebalancing, and a diversified portfolio of low-cost ETFs matter more than a slick interface. Ask what your money actually buys: most robo-advisors build around broad index ETFs, which is exactly what a beginner wants.

Extras that keep you consistent

Round-ups (spare change from purchases), fractional shares, dividend reinvestment, and educational content all increase the odds you'll keep contributing. A slightly higher fee is worth it if the app makes you stick with it.

Best overall robo-advisor for beginners

  • Betterment — A strong default for most beginners. Low minimum, automatic deposits, a simple goal-based interface, and a management fee that's a fraction of a percent per year. Great if you want a set-and-forget portfolio.

Best micro-investing app with more control

A quick note if you're in the EU

US-based apps like Acorns and Stash generally aren't available to EU residents. European beginners can look at Moneyfarm, Scalable Capital, or Trade Republic, which offer managed portfolios or low-cost fractional investing depending on your country. The selection criteria stay the same: minimums, total cost, and automation.

Acorns vs Stash: Which Is Better for Beginners?

This is the comparison most people search for, and the honest answer is that they solve slightly different problems.

Choose Acorns if: you want the simplest possible experience. Round-ups turn everyday spending into investments, portfolios are fully managed, and you barely have to make a decision. It's ideal for someone who wants to start with $5 to $20 and build the habit first. The trade-off is a flat monthly fee that's expensive relative to a small balance.

Choose Stash if: you want to learn while you invest. Stash offers guided portfolios plus the option to buy individual stocks and ETFs, along with educational content. It also uses a flat monthly fee, so it rewards you for building a larger balance.

Consider a percentage-fee robo-advisor instead if: you're planning to invest $500 or more and want the lowest ongoing cost. On a $1,000 balance, a 0.25% annual fee is $2.50 per year — far less than a monthly subscription.

How to Start Investing With $100: Step by Step

  1. Build a small emergency buffer first. Investing is for money you won't need for at least five years. If $100 is your last $100, keep it in savings.
  2. Pick one app, not three. Splitting $100 across multiple accounts creates fees without diversification benefits.
  3. Choose a diversified portfolio. A target-date-style or broad index ETF portfolio is fine. You don't need to pick winning stocks.
  4. Fund the account and invest the full amount. Don't leave cash sitting uninvested.
  5. Set a recurring deposit. Even $25 per month matters more than your starting amount.
  6. Turn on dividend reinvestment. It compounds without any effort from you.
  7. Check in quarterly, not daily. Daily checking leads to tinkering, and tinkering leads to worse returns.

Common Beginner Mistakes to Avoid

  • Choosing an app based on fees alone. A cheap app you never use is worse than a slightly pricier one you fund automatically.
  • Panic-selling during a dip. Volatility is normal. If a 20% drop would make you sell, you're invested above your risk tolerance.
  • Ignoring the expense ratio. A management fee is only part of your cost; the funds inside your portfolio charge their own fees.
  • Chasing a hot stock with your first $100. Concentrated bets are for money you can afford to lose entirely.
  • Forgetting taxes. In a taxable account, selling triggers tax consequences. Long-term holding keeps things simple.

FAQ

What's the best robo-advisor for a complete beginner with $100?

For most people, a low-minimum, percentage-fee robo-advisor like Betterment or Fidelity Go. You'll pay very little on a small balance while getting a diversified, automatically managed portfolio. If you specifically want round-ups, Acorns is the better fit.

Can you really start investing with $100?

Yes. Fractional shares mean you don't need hundreds of dollars to buy into diversified funds. The account minimums at most beginner-friendly apps are $0 to $5, and the real determinant of your results is how consistently you keep contributing.

Are micro-investing apps like Acorns and Stash worth the monthly fee?

They're worth it if the round-ups and simple interface keep you investing, and less worth it once your balance grows large enough that a percentage-based advisor would cost less. Reassess your app choice every time your balance doubles.

The Bottom Line

You don't need a big balance to start — you need one account, one diversified portfolio, and an automatic deposit. Open an account with the app that matches how hands-on you want to be, put your $100 to work, and set a recurring contribution you can genuinely afford. Then leave it alone and let time do the heavy lifting.
