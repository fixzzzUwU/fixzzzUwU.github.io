Best Investing Apps for Beginners to Start with $100 or Less

You don't need thousands of dollars to start investing. In fact, with just $100, you can open an account, buy fractional shares of major companies, and build a habit that could grow into a serious portfolio over time. The only real challenge is choosing the right app. That's why we've put together this buying guide to the best investing apps for beginners — all of which work with $100 or less.

Can You Really Start Investing with $100?

Yes, absolutely.

Most modern brokerage apps have removed account minimums entirely. You can open a Robinhood account with zero dollars and fund it with $100 whenever you're ready. Acorns and Stash let you start with just a few dollars. And thanks to fractional shares, you don't need to buy a full share of an expensive stock like Amazon or Google — you can invest $25, $10, or even $5 into a company.

The real value of starting with $100 isn't the money itself. It's the routine. Investing a small amount consistently teaches you how markets move, how dividends work, and how your own risk tolerance feels in practice. That education is worth more than the dollar amount.

What to Look for in a Micro-Investing App

Before choosing an app, it helps to know what actually matters when you're working with a small account.

Fees and Minimums

This is the most important factor for a $100 portfolio. Some micro-investing apps charge a flat monthly subscription fee. Acorns, for example, charges around $3 per month, which is fine once your balance is in the thousands, but it can eat a noticeable chunk of a $100 balance. Other apps, like Robinhood and SoFi Invest, charge $0 monthly fees and $0 per trade, making them a better fit for very small accounts.

Fractional Shares

A fractional share lets you invest a specific dollar amount rather than buying a whole share. This is essential when you only have $100. Without fractional shares, you might be limited to low-priced stocks, and you'd miss out on some of the best-performing companies simply because a single share costs more than your entire budget.

Automation and Round-Ups

Many beginner apps let you automate your investing. You can set up recurring deposits from your paycheck, or use a round-up feature that invests your spare change from everyday purchases. Automation removes the discipline problem — if the money is invested before you can spend it, you're much more likely to stick with the habit.

Educational Content

The best investing apps for beginners include learning tools built right into the platform. Look for apps that explain investing terms in plain English, offer articles or videos, and help you understand why your portfolio looks the way it doeschers. Education is what separates someone who invests from someone who simply gambles on ticker symbols.

Acorns vs Robinhood: Which Should You Choose?

No comparison comes up more often in the micro-investing space than Acorns vs Robinhood. Both are excellent apps, but they serve completely different habits.

Acorns is for people who want investing to happen automatically. You connect your card, round up your purchases, and Acorns invests the change into a professionally managed ETF portfolio. It's a true hands-off experience. The trade-off is the monthly fee, which can be significant on a small balance.

Robinhood is for people who want control. You open the app, research stocks, and place your own trades. There are no commissions and no monthly fees, which makes it cheaper for a $100 account. The trade-off is that you have to make your own decisions, and the app makes it very easy to trade frequently, which can lead to mistakes when you're just starting out.

So, which should a beginner choose? If you want to build wealth without thinking about it, start with Acorns. If you want to learn the mechanics of the market and take an active role, start with Robinhood. And there's nothing wrong with opening both and seeing which one you actually open more often.

How to Start Investing with $100: A Simple Four-Step Plan

Knowing which app to use is only half the equation. Here's how to start investing with $100 in a way that sets you up for long-term success.

Step 1: Pick one app and fund it

Choose one app from the recommendations above. Link your bank account and transfer $100. Most apps will have the money available within a few business days.

Step 2: Build a simple starter portfolio

Put a large portion of your $100 into a broad-market ETF that tracks the S&P 500. This gives you instant diversification across hundreds of companies. Then, if you want, use the remaining money to buy fractional shares of one or two companies you believe in.

Step 3: Set up a recurring deposit

Even $10 per week can make a big difference over a few years. Automate it so you invest before you have a chance to spend the money. Consistent contributions matter more than the size of each one.

Step 4: Ignore the daily ups and downs

Your $100 will go up and down in value, sometimes by several dollars in a single week. That's normal. The goal isn't to make a quick profit — it's to build a habit and let compound growth work over years, not days.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is $100 enough to start investing?

Yes. Apps like Robinhood, Acorns, and SoFi Invest have no minimum account balance, and fractional shares let you invest any dollar amount. $100 is enough to build a diversified starter portfolio, especially if you pair it with small recurring deposits.

Are micro-investing apps safe for beginners?

Yes, most are regulated brokerages and protect your investments through SIPC insurance up to certain limits. However, investing always carries market risk — your portfolio can lose value. The apps themselves are safe, but the stocks and ETFs inside them can go up or down.

What is the difference between Acorns and Robinhood?

Acorns is an automated robo-advisor that invests your spare change into a managed portfolio of ETFs for a monthly fee. Robinhood is a self-directed brokerage that lets you choose your own stocks and ETFs with $0 commissions and no monthly fees. Acorns is for passive investors; Robinhood is for active investors.
