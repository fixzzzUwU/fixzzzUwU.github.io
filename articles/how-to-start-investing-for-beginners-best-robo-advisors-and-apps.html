How to Start Investing for Beginners: Best Robo-Advisors and Apps

If you've been meaning to start investing but keep putting it off, you're not alone. The two biggest blockers for beginners are usually the same: it feels complicated, and it feels expensive. The good news is that neither is really true anymore. Automated platforms have removed most of the guesswork, and many of them let you open an account with the kind of money you'd spend on a takeout dinner.

This guide walks through how to start investing for beginners, what to look for in a platform, and which robo-advisors and micro-investing apps are worth your attention in the US and Europe.

Why beginners are choosing automated investing

A robo-advisor is a digital platform that builds and manages a diversified portfolio for you. You answer a short questionnaire about your goals, timeline, and comfort with risk, and the platform handles the rest: choosing investments, buying them, and rebalancing over time.

That automation matters for two reasons. First, it removes the emotional decisions that cause most beginners to buy high and sell low. Second, it removes the need to research individual stocks or funds before you've learned the basics.

Micro-investing apps take the same idea and lower the barrier further. They let you start with small amounts, buy fractional shares, and in some cases invest your spare change from everyday purchases.

How to start investing with 100 dollars (or less)

Before you pick an app, sort out a few fundamentals.

Build a small cushion first. Investing is for money you won't need for at least a few years. Keep an emergency fund in a savings account so a surprise expense doesn't force you to sell investments at a bad time.

Clear high-interest debt. If you're carrying credit card balances, paying those down is usually a better guaranteed return than anything the market offers.

Decide what you're investing for. Retirement, a house deposit, and a general "grow my money" goal call for different time horizons and different levels of risk.

Choose the account type. In the US, that usually means a taxable brokerage account, a Roth IRA, or a traditional IRA. In the EU and UK, look at a general investment account, an ISA (UK), or a tax-advantaged retirement product in your country.

Once those are settled, $100 is genuinely enough to begin. Fractional investing means you don't need to buy a whole share of anything, and most robo-advisors have no account minimum at all.

Best picks: robo-advisors and micro-investing apps for beginners

The right platform depends on how much you want to be involved. Here are the categories worth comparing, with the names most beginners end up weighing.

  • **Wealthfront — similar automated approach with a strong focus on portfolio construction and automated tax optimization for US clients.

Acorns vs Betterment: which one fits you?

This is the comparison beginners search for most, and the answer comes down to your starting point.

Choose Acorns if: you're starting with very little, you want investing to happen automatically out of your normal spending, and you'd rather not think about portfolio allocation at all.

Choose Betterment if: you have a lump sum or a steady monthly amount to invest, you want goal-based planning and a more structured portfolio, and you're comfortable with a slightly more involved setup.

The practical difference is friction. Acorns is designed to make investing invisible; Betterment is designed to make it intentional. Neither is "better" in the abstract — it depends on whether your problem is forgetting to invest or not knowing what to invest in.

Passive investing apps vs dividend investing for beginners

Most robo-advisors follow a passive investing approach: they hold broad, low-cost index funds and don't try to beat the market. This is generally the right starting point for beginners because it's cheap, diversified, and easy to stick with.

Dividend investing for beginners is a different strategy. Instead of only chasing price growth, you buy companies or funds that pay out part of their profits regularly. Dividend-focused funds can be a reasonable addition once you have a base portfolio, and some robo-advisors now offer dividend-tilted portfolios. But be careful: chasing high dividend yields can mean taking on more risk than you realize, and dividends are not guaranteed. For most people just starting out, a broad diversified portfolio first, dividend strategy later, is the more sensible order.

A quick checklist before you sign up

  • Fees: Look at the annual advisory fee, the underlying fund fees, and any account or withdrawal charges. Small percentages add up over decades.
  • Minimum deposit: Many platforms now have none, but confirm before you start.
  • Investment options: Broad index funds and ETFs are what you want for a core portfolio.
  • Automatic deposits: Recurring contributions matter more than picking the perfect platform.
  • Account types: Make sure the platform supports the tax-advantaged account available in your country.
  • Withdrawal rules: Know how to get your money out and whether there are penalties.
  • Regulation and protection: Check that the platform is regulated in your jurisdiction and understand what investor protection applies.

Conclusion

Starting to invest is less about finding a secret strategy and more about removing friction. Pick a regulated platform with low fees, set up an automatic monthly contribution — even a small one — and let diversification and time do the work.

If you want the simplest possible entry, start with a micro-investing app. If you have a bit more to invest and want structure, a robo-advisor is the better fit. Either way, the most important step is the first deposit.

FAQ

What's the best robo-advisor for beginners with very little money?

Platforms with no account minimum are the best fit. Acorns, Stash, and M1 Finance in the US, and Trade Republic or Scalable Capital in Europe, all let you start with small amounts. Compare fees before choosing, since a low balance makes percentage fees more noticeable.

Can I really start investing with $100?

Yes. Fractional shares mean $100 can buy a diversified slice of an index fund rather than a single stock. Many robo-advisors have no minimum, so $100 is enough to open an account and set up a recurring contribution.

Is Acorns or Betterment better for a complete beginner?

Acorns is easier if you want investing to happen automatically from spare change and you're starting with very little. Betterment is better if you have a lump sum or a monthly amount to invest and want goal-based planning with a more structured portfolio.
