What to Look For in a Beginner Investing App

Before comparing names, it helps to know what actually matters. Five things:

  • Low, transparent costs. Look for $0 commissions on stock and ETF trades, low expense ratios (broad index ETFs often sit under 0.10%), and no hidden account fees. Robo-advisors typically charge an annual management fee of around 0.25% of your balance.
  • Fractional shares. This lets you buy a slice of an expensive stock or ETF with whatever dollar amount you have.
  • Automatic investing. Recurring transfers and auto-invest features are what turn a one-time deposit into real wealth.
  • A portfolio you understand. If you can't explain what you own in one sentence, it's the wrong product for a beginner.
  • Regulatory protection. In the US, make sure the broker is a member of SIPC (which covers securities up to $500,000 if the brokerage fails). In the EU, look for entities regulated under local financial authorities and deposit-guarantee schemes.

1. M1 Finance — Best for Hands-Off DIY Investors

M1 is built around "Pies" — visual portfolios where you set target percentages for each stock or ETF, then let the platform allocate your deposits accordingly. You decide the recipe; M1 does the slicing.

Why beginners like it:

  • Commission-free investing with no management fee on self-directed accounts
  • Automatic investing on a schedule you choose
  • Dynamic rebalancing when you add money
  • Fractional shares, so every dollar gets put to work

Worth knowing: M1 is designed for long-term buy-and-hold investors. If you want to day trade or trade options actively, it isn't the right fit. Also check current pricing tiers, since features differ between the free and premium versions.

2. Acorns — Best for Round-Up Saving

Acorns connects to your debit or credit cards and rounds up purchases to the nearest dollar, investing the difference into a diversified portfolio. It's the closest thing to "investing without noticing."

Why beginners like it:

  • Spare-change round-ups turn everyday spending into investments
  • Pre-built portfolios matched to your risk tolerance and timeline
  • Recurring contributions you can set and forget
  • Extras like retirement and custodial accounts

Worth knowing: Acorns charges a flat monthly fee rather than a percentage, which is cheap on larger balances but comparatively expensive if you're only investing a few hundred dollars. It's a habit-building tool first and a low-cost brokerage second.

3. Stash — Best for Learning While You Invest

Stash pairs investing with short educational lessons, which makes it a solid pick if you want to understand what you're buying rather than just tap "invest."

Why beginners like it:

Worth knowing: Like Acorns, Stash uses a monthly subscription model. If your balance is small, that fee eats into returns — so plan to grow your contributions over time, not just park $50 there forever.

Acorns vs Stash: Which One Fits You?

This is one of the most common beginner questions, and the honest answer is that they're more similar than different. Both use a flat monthly fee, both offer fractional investing, and both are aimed at people who want a simple, guided experience.

Choose Acorns if your main goal is to build the habit of investing. Round-ups are its killer feature, and the automatic nature of it means you're less likely to overthink.

Choose Stash if you want to learn as you go. The educational content and themed portfolios give you more context about what you're actually buying.

If neither fee structure appeals to you — say you're planning to invest several thousand dollars — a self-directed account like M1 Finance or Fidelity will usually cost you less over time.

How to Start Dividend Investing as a Beginner

Dividend investing sounds advanced, but the core idea is simple: you buy shares of companies or funds that pay out part of their profits to shareholders, and you collect those payments — usually quarterly.

A beginner-friendly approach:

  1. Start with dividend ETFs, not individual stocks. A dividend-focused ETF holds dozens or hundreds of companies, so one bad pick doesn't sink you.
  2. Turn on dividend reinvestment (DRIP). Instead of taking the cash, you automatically buy more shares. Those new shares pay dividends too, which compounds over time.
  3. Focus on dividend growth, not just yield. A very high yield can be a warning sign that the payout is at risk. Companies with a long history of raising dividends tend to be more reliable.
  4. Hold in a tax-advantaged account when possible. In the US, that means a Roth IRA or traditional IRA; in the EU, look at your country's equivalent retirement or tax-sheltered wrapper.
  5. Reinvest for years, not months. Dividend investing rewards patience. The income snowball is small at first and gets interesting later.

Passive Income Investing Apps: What They Can and Can't Do

Apps marketed as "passive income" tools usually mean one of three things: automated contributions, dividend reinvestment, or lending/alternative income products. All three can work — but none of them are truly passive in the sense of "money with zero effort."

What they can do: automate deposits, reinvest dividends, rebalance your portfolio, and remove the emotional decision-making that trips up most beginners.

What they can't do: guarantee returns, replace an emergency fund, or turn $200 into a livable income. Anyone promising that is selling something else.

The realistic version of passive income investing looks like this: set up an automatic transfer, buy a diversified portfolio, reinvest dividends, and check in a couple of times a year. That's it.

FAQ

What's the best investing app for beginners with very little money?

Look for no account minimums, fractional shares, and $0 commissions. M1 Finance, Fidelity, and Betterment all let you start with a small amount. Acorns and Stash are also accessible, but their monthly fees matter more when your balance is small, so weigh that against the convenience.

Can I start dividend investing with $100?

Yes. Fractional shares and low-cost dividend ETFs mean $100 can buy you a diversified slice of the dividend market. The key is to keep adding regularly and reinvest the dividends rather than spending them.

Are investing apps safe?

Reputable US brokers are SIPC members, which protects securities if the brokerage fails (it does not protect against market losses). In the EU, check that the provider is regulated by a national financial authority and covered by the relevant investor compensation scheme. The bigger risk for most beginners isn't app security — it's panic-selling during a downturn.

The Bottom Line

The best investing app for beginners is the one you'll actually use consistently. Pick a low-cost option, automate your contributions, reinvest your dividends, and leave it alone. Time in the market does the heavy lifting — the app is just the vehicle.
