Best Investing Apps for Beginners: Compare Fees, Features, and Bonuses

Starting to invest with a small amount of money used to feel pointless. Brokerages wanted thousands of dollars upfront, trades cost $10 a pop, and buying a single share of a well-known company could eat your entire budget. That's no longer the case.

Today, a beginner can open an account in minutes, deposit $100, and own a slice of hundreds of companies through fractional shares or a diversified portfolio managed by an app. The hard part isn't access anymore — it's choosing which app deserves your money.

This guide compares the best investing apps for beginners on the things that actually matter: fees, minimums, automation, and sign-up bonuses. No hype, no jargon, just a clear look at how the main options differ.

What to Look for in a Beginner Investing App

Before comparing brand names, get clear on the four factors that separate a good beginner app from an expensive one.

Fees That Actually Matter

Beginners often fixate on commissions, but most major US apps dropped stock trading commissions years ago. The fees that quietly drain small accounts are:

  • Advisory fees — what robo-advisors charge to manage your portfolio, commonly around 0.25% of assets per year.
  • Expense ratios — the internal cost of the funds you hold, often as low as 0.03% for index funds.
  • Account fees — monthly subscription charges on apps like Acorns or Stash.
  • Foreign transaction or conversion fees — relevant if you're investing from the EU.

On a $100 balance, a $3 monthly fee is a 36% annual drag. That's why subscription-based apps make more sense once your balance grows.

Minimums and Account Types

Some apps require nothing to start; others want $5, $25, or more. Check whether the app supports a taxable brokerage account, a Roth IRA, or both. If you're investing for retirement, opening an IRA inside the same app keeps things simple.

Fractional Shares and Automation

Fractional shares let you buy $10 of a $400 stock. This matters enormously when you're starting small, because it means you're never forced to choose between diversification and budget. Automatic recurring deposits are the other feature to prioritize — consistent contributions beat perfectly timed ones.

How to Start Investing With $100

You don't need a strategy degree. Here's a practical sequence:

  1. Decide your goal. Retirement, a house fund, or general wealth-building all point to different account types.
  2. Pick one app. Splitting $100 across three platforms makes each position too small to matter.
  3. Choose a diversified fund. A broad market index ETF gives you exposure to hundreds of companies in one purchase.
  4. Set up an automatic deposit. Even $25 a month compounds meaningfully over a decade.
  5. Leave it alone. Passive investing apps work because you don't tinker with them during market dips.

Fractional Shares Apps: Why They Matter for Small Accounts

Fractional share platforms remove the biggest structural disadvantage beginners face. Without them, a $100 budget might buy one share of a single company — the opposite of diversification.

With fractional investing, that same $100 can be spread across an index fund, a few individual stocks, or a themed portfolio. You keep full ownership of the underlying shares; the brokerage just holds them in fractions internally. Most major beginner apps now support this, including Robinhood, M1 Finance, Public, and Stash.

Bonuses and Promotions: What to Know

Many apps run referral bonuses or deposit-match promotions. A few things to keep in mind:

  • Read the terms. Most bonuses require a minimum deposit or a holding period.
  • Don't chase bonuses over fit. A $50 bonus isn't worth a platform with high recurring fees.
  • Watch the tax side. Referral bonuses are typically taxable income in the US.
  • Compare like for like. A one-time bonus and an ongoing fee difference aren't the same thing.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I really start investing with $100?

Yes. Most beginner apps have no minimum, and fractional shares let you build a diversified position with a small deposit. The key is choosing a low-fee platform so costs don't eat your returns.

Are robo-advisors better than picking stocks myself?

For most beginners, yes. Robo-advisors automate diversification and rebalancing, which removes the two most common mistakes: concentrating in one stock and panic-selling during downturns. If you enjoy research and want control, a self-directed fractional share app is a reasonable alternative.

What's the best passive investing app for a complete beginner?

If you want zero decisions, a robo-advisor like Betterment or Wealthfront is the simplest path. If you want to choose your own funds but still automate deposits, M1 Finance offers a good middle ground.

Final Thoughts

The best investing app for beginners isn't the one with the flashiest bonus — it's the one with low ongoing costs that you'll actually use consistently. Start with a platform that supports fractional shares, has no minimum, and lets you automate contributions. Open the account, buy a broad index fund, set a recurring deposit, and let time do the heavy lifting.

Compare the picks above, check the current fee schedules, and choose the one that matches how hands-on you want to be. Then start small and stay consistent — that's the whole strategy.
