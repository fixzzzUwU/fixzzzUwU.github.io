Seed Starting Kits Compared: Best Indoor Greenhouses and Grow Lights

Starting seeds indoors comes down to managing four things: light, warmth, moisture, and airflow. Get those right and almost any vegetable will germinate on a windowsill or in a spare room. Get them wrong and you end up with leggy, pale seedlings that never recover.

The problem is that "seed starting kit" means wildly different things depending on who's selling it. Some kits are just plastic trays and a dome. Others bundle a heat mat, a humidity cover, and a full LED grow light on a stand. This comparison breaks down the main setups, explains what actually matters, and points you toward the best pick for your space and budget.

What Actually Matters in a Seed Starting Kit

Trays, Cells, and Drainage

Cell size drives root development. Small cells (72 to 128 per tray) suit lettuce, herbs, and brassicas that you'll transplant quickly. Larger cells (12 to 24 per tray) give tomatoes, peppers, and squash room to build roots before they go outside.

Look for trays with individual drainage holes and a bottom water reservoir or a sturdy 1020 flat underneath. Thin, flimsy trays crack after one season. Silicone or thick recycled plastic cells pop out seedlings without tearing roots, and soil blocks are worth considering if you want to skip plastic entirely.

Grow Lights: Spectrum, Wattage, and Coverage

This is where most kits cut corners. A "grow light" that's a single weak LED strip will keep seedlings alive but won't keep them stocky. What you want:

  • Full-spectrum white LEDs rather than blurple. Easier to judge plant health by eye.
  • Enough output to sit close to the canopy. Seedlings need roughly 14 to 16 hours of light per day.
  • Adjustable height or a hanging kit, so you can raise the light as plants grow.
  • Coverage that matches your tray footprint. A 2-foot bar light suits one 1020 tray; a 4-foot fixture handles a shelf.

Humidity Domes and Indoor Greenhouses

Seeds need consistent moisture and warmth to germinate. A clear dome traps humidity and reduces how often you mist. Once seedlings emerge, the dome comes off — leaving it on invites damping-off disease.

A small indoor greenhouse for seedlings — typically a zip-up shelving unit with a clear cover — does the same job at a larger scale. It holds several trays, keeps humidity stable, and protects against drafts. If you're starting more than three or four trays, a greenhouse unit is more practical than stacking domes.

Heat Mats and Airflow

Most vegetable seeds germinate fastest at soil temperatures between roughly 70°F and 80°F. A heat mat under the tray makes a real difference in a cool basement or garage. Buy a mat with a thermostat if you can; uncontrolled mats can cook trays.

Airflow matters just as much. A small clip fan moving air across seedlings strengthens stems and discourages mold. Many all-in-one kits skip this, so budget for a fan separately.

The practical takeaway: buy the light first. Trays and domes are cheap and interchangeable. Light is the component that determines whether your seedlings thrive.

How to Use Your Kit Without Killing Your Seedlings

  1. Fill cells loosely and water from the bottom. Tamping soil too firmly suffocates roots.
  2. Keep the light two to four inches above the canopy. Raise it as plants grow; distance is how you prevent stretching.
  3. Run lights 14 to 16 hours, then give darkness. Seedlings need a night cycle.
  4. Remove domes at first sign of green. Germination humidity becomes disease humidity once leaves appear.
  5. Bottom-water once seedlings are up. Keeps foliage dry and reduces mold.
  6. Brush your hand over seedlings daily or run a fan on low. Gentle movement builds sturdier stems.

FAQ

Do I really need a grow light to start seeds indoors?

A bright south-facing window can work for lettuce and herbs, but most vegetable seedlings grown on a windowsill stretch toward the glass and end up leggy. A basic full-spectrum LED bar costs less than replacing a failed tray of starts and gives you consistent, controllable light.

How long should seedlings stay under a grow light each day?

Aim for 14 to 16 hours of light followed by 8 to 10 hours of darkness. Using a timer makes this automatic. More than 18 hours offers little benefit and can stress some plants.

Can I use an indoor greenhouse for seedlings without a grow light?

Yes, if it sits in strong natural light — but a covered greenhouse diffuses light, so seedlings often need supplemental lighting anyway. If you're buying one of these units, plan on adding a shelf light or bar light for best results.

Conclusion

The best seed starting kit isn't the one with the most pieces — it's the one that gives you adequate light and steady warmth. Start with a setup that includes a height-adjustable full-spectrum light and a tray with real drainage. Add a heat mat if your starting space runs cool, and a small fan once seedlings emerge. Scale up to an indoor greenhouse for seedlings only when you're regularly filling more than a couple of trays.

Buy the light first, keep it close to the canopy, and take the dome off as soon as you see green. That's most of the skill involved — the rest is just watering.
