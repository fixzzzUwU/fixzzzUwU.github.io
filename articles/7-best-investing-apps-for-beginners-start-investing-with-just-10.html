7 Best Investing Apps for Beginners: Start Investing With Just $10

Ten years ago, opening a brokerage account meant a few thousand dollars, a stack of paperwork, and a phone call. Today you can buy a slice of an S&P 500 ETF from your phone during a lunch break — with $10, or even $1.

That's the good news. The confusing news is that there are now dozens of apps competing for your first deposit, and they all claim to be the best investing app for beginners. Some are built for spare-change round-ups. Some are robo-advisors that manage everything for you. Some are full trading platforms with charts that will make your eyes glaze over.

This guide walks through what actually matters when you're starting out, then breaks down seven solid options for first-time investors, side hustlers, and anyone who wants their money working instead of sitting.

What to Look For in a Beginner Investing App

Before comparing logos, compare these four things. They'll tell you more about whether an app fits you than any marketing page will.

Minimums and fees

Your entry point should be low — ideally $0 to open an account and $1–$10 to make your first investment. Watch for two fee types: monthly subscription fees (common with micro-investing apps) and annual advisory fees (common with robo-advisors, usually a small percentage of your balance). A $3 monthly fee is trivial on a $10,000 portfolio and painful on a $200 one. Do the math on your actual starting balance before you commit.

Fractional shares and automation

Fractional shares let you buy part of a stock or ETF instead of a whole one, which is what makes a $10 start possible. Automation — recurring deposits, automatic round-ups, auto-rebalancing — matters just as much, because the single biggest predictor of beginner success is consistency, not stock picking.

Education and guardrails

The best beginner platforms explain what you're buying in plain English and nudge you toward diversified funds rather than meme stocks. If an app's onboarding is a leaderboard of yesterday's biggest gainers, it's designed for trading, not investing.

Account types

Most apps offer a standard taxable brokerage account. Some also offer IRAs (traditional and Roth) and even custodial accounts. If retirement is your goal, prioritize apps with IRA support so you don't have to move later.

Best Picks: 7 Investing Apps for Beginners

1. Acorns — best for spare-change round-ups

Acorns links to your debit or credit card, rounds up every purchase to the nearest dollar, and invests the difference into a diversified portfolio built from ETFs. You can also set recurring deposits. It's the closest thing to "set it and forget it" for people who hate thinking about money.

  • Acorns
  • Best for: hands-off investors who want saving built into daily spending
  • Standout feature: Round-Ups and automatic portfolio rebalancing
  • Watch out for: a monthly subscription fee that makes more sense once your balance grows

2. Stash — best for learning while you invest

Stash pairs fractional investing with short, jargon-free lessons on investing basics, plus themed portfolios if you want to invest around your values. It's a good middle ground between "do it all for me" and "here's a blank search bar."

  • Stash
  • Best for: beginners who want education alongside their portfolio
  • Standout feature: guided lessons and curated portfolio options
  • Watch out for: monthly plan pricing — check the current tiers before signing up

3. Robinhood — best for commission-free stock and ETF trading

Robinhood popularized zero-commission trading and a clean, simple interface. You can buy fractional shares, set recurring investments, and open an IRA. It's a legitimate starting point if you want to pick your own ETFs — just resist the urge to treat it like a casino.

  • Robinhood
  • Best for: beginners who want to choose their own investments with no minimum
  • Standout feature: fractional shares, recurring buys, and no account minimum
  • Watch out for: an interface that makes active trading feel fun — it usually isn't

4. Betterment — best robo-advisor for goal-based investing

Betterment is a robo-advisor: you answer a few questions about goals and risk tolerance, and it builds and manages a diversified ETF portfolio for you. It supports multiple goals (emergency fund, retirement, a house) in one dashboard, and offers tax-efficient features on larger balances.

  • Betterment
  • Best for: beginners who want professional-style portfolio management without a human advisor
  • Standout feature: goal-based buckets and automatic rebalancing
  • Watch out for: an annual advisory fee on top of the fund expenses

5. Wealthfront — best for automation and extra features

Wealthfront is another strong robo-advisor, with automated investing, dividend reinvestment, and a range of planning tools. It's a good fit if you like the idea of a dashboard that shows your progress toward specific financial goals rather than a wall of ticker symbols.

  • Wealthfront
  • Best for: long-term, automated investors who want planning tools included
  • Standout feature: automated portfolio management with goal tracking
  • Watch out for: the advisory fee, which matters more on small balances

6. M1 Finance — best for building your own "pie"

M1 lets you create a custom portfolio — a "pie" — of stocks and ETFs with your own percentage weights, then automates deposits and rebalancing to keep it on target. It's a smart pick if you want control over what you own but don't want to place manual trades every month.

  • M1 Finance
  • Best for: beginners who want a rules-based, self-directed portfolio
  • Standout feature: customizable pies with automatic rebalancing
  • Watch out for: less hand-holding than a robo-advisor

7. Public — best for fractional shares plus community

Public offers commission-free fractional investing in stocks and ETFs, plus a social layer where you can see what other investors are holding and why. The community aspect is genuinely useful for learning — as long as you treat it as research, not a signal service.

  • Public
  • Best for: beginners who learn better from discussion than from textbooks
  • Standout feature: fractional shares with a built-in investing community
  • Watch out for: herd behavior — popularity isn't a strategy

Micro-Investing Apps vs. Robo-Advisors: A Quick Comparison

| | Micro-investing apps | Robo-advisors |

|---|---|---|

| Typical minimum | $0–$5 | $0–$500 |

| Who manages it | You, with automation | The platform |

| Main cost | Monthly subscription | Annual advisory fee |

| Best for | Building the habit | Hands-off long-term growth |

If you're starting with $10 and your main problem is consistency, a micro-investing app is usually the better first step. If you already have a few thousand dollars and want it managed, a robo-advisor earns its fee.

How to Start Investing With $100

You don't need a strategy meeting. You need four steps.

  1. Pick one app from the list above that matches how much effort you want to put in.
  2. Open the account and link a bank account — expect a quick identity check.
  3. Choose a diversified starting point, like a broad market ETF or a pre-built portfolio. Avoid single stocks for your first deposit.
  4. Automate a recurring deposit — even $25 a month — and leave it alone.

That last step is the whole game. A $100 starting balance plus consistent contributions beats a $1,000 balance you never add to.

One honest note: investing involves risk, including losing money, and investment accounts aren't FDIC-insured like bank deposits. Only invest money you won't need for at least a few years.

Passive Income Apps: What They Can and Can't Do

Micro-investing apps get marketed as "passive income apps," and that framing deserves a caveat. Dividends and long-term market growth can produce passive income eventually — but it takes years and a growing balance, not weeks. These apps make investing easier and more automatic. They don't make it fast. Treat any app promising quick returns as a red flag.

FAQ

What's the best investing app for beginners with only $10?

Any app offering fractional shares and no account minimum will work — Acorns, Stash, Robinhood, M1 Finance, and Public are all reasonable starting points. Choose based on how much involvement you want, not on which one has the flashiest features.

Can I really start investing with $100?

Yes. With fractional shares, $100 can buy a diversified mix of ETFs rather than a single stock. The bigger factor is what you do next: setting up an automatic monthly deposit matters more than the size of your first one.

Are robo-advisors worth it compared to micro-investing apps?

Robo-advisors like Betterment and Wealthfront charge an annual fee but handle diversification, rebalancing, and tax efficiency for you. They tend to make more sense once your balance is large enough that the fee is a small fraction of your potential gains. For a first $10 or $100, a micro-investing app is usually the cheaper way to build the habit.

The Bottom Line

The best investing app for beginners isn't the one with the lowest fee or the slickest design — it's the one you'll actually keep funding. Start with $10, automate a small deposit, choose something diversified, and let time do the heavy lifting. You can always upgrade to a robo-advisor later once your balance and your confidence have grown.
