Best Investing Apps for Beginners: Acorns vs Stash vs Betterment Compared

You don't need thousands of dollars to start investing. You need a few dollars, a phone, and an app that doesn't make you feel dumb for asking basic questions.

That's the whole pitch behind micro-investing apps and robo-advisors. They lower the barrier that used to keep young investors out: high minimums, confusing fund choices, and the fear of picking the "wrong" stock. But Acorns, Stash, and Betterment solve that problem in three very different ways — and the one that's right for you depends less on returns and more on how you actually behave with money.

This comparison breaks down what each app does well, where it falls short, and which one fits your situation.

What Actually Separates These Three Apps

All three let you start small. After that, they diverge fast.

  • Acorns is built around automation and spare change. You connect a card, it rounds up your purchases, and it invests the difference. You make almost no decisions.
  • Stash is built around learning. It offers fractional shares, themed collections, and educational content so you can pick some of your own investments.
  • Betterment is a true robo-advisor. You set a goal, answer a risk questionnaire, and it builds and manages a diversified ETF portfolio for you.

The factors that matter most for beginners

Before you download anything, compare these five things:

  1. Minimum to start — can you begin with $5 or do you need hundreds?
  2. Fee structure — flat monthly subscription versus a percentage of your balance. This matters enormously when your balance is small.
  3. Automation — does it invest for you, or do you have to log in and decide?
  4. Control — do you want to choose holdings, or would you rather not think about it?
  5. Account types — taxable brokerage only, or also IRAs and retirement options?

Fees are the big one. A flat monthly fee is cheap on a $5,000 balance and expensive on a $50 balance. A percentage fee is the opposite. Keep that in mind as you read.

Acorns: Best for Round-Ups and Autopilot Saving

How it works

Acorns links to your debit or credit card and rounds every purchase up to the next dollar, investing the spare change into a diversified portfolio of ETFs chosen for you. You can also set recurring deposits, and the platform offers retirement accounts alongside its standard investing account.

Who it's for

People who have trouble saving because they never notice small amounts leaving their account. If you've ever thought "I don't have money to invest," Acorns is designed to prove you wrong — quietly, in the background.

Watch out for

The monthly subscription fee. On a tiny balance, that fee can represent a meaningful percentage of what you've invested. Acorns makes the most sense once you're also making recurring deposits, not just relying on round-ups. You also get limited control over what you own.

Stash: Best for Learning as You Invest

How it works

Stash lets you buy fractional shares, so a $5 deposit can go into a piece of a fund or a stock. It groups investments into themed collections, offers guided portfolios for people who don't want to pick, and includes a substantial amount of plain-English educational content. Retirement accounts are available too.

Who it's for

Beginners who want to understand what they own. If you're the type who reads the instructions before assembling furniture, Stash scratches that itch. It's a good middle ground between total autopilot and going it alone in a full brokerage.

Watch out for

More choice means more chances to make emotional decisions. Buying individual stocks — even fractional ones — carries more risk than a broad diversified fund. And Stash uses tiered monthly subscriptions, so check which tier you actually need before signing up.

Betterment: Best Robo-Advisor for Hands-Off Portfolios

How it works

You tell Betterment what you're investing for — retirement, a safety net, a big purchase — and answer questions about your timeline and risk tolerance. It builds a diversified ETF portfolio, automatically rebalances it, and offers features like automatic dividend reinvestment and tax-loss harvesting on higher tiers. It supports individual and retirement accounts.

Who it's for

Anyone who wants a real, goal-based portfolio without picking investments. Betterment also scales better than the subscription apps: it charges an annual advisory fee based on your balance, so the cost grows with your money rather than sitting flat.

Watch out for

That percentage fee never goes away, and it grows as your balance grows. Betterment also isn't trying to be fun — there are no themed collections or meme-stock shopping sprees. For most beginners, that's a feature.

Acorns vs Stash: Which Micro-Investing App Wins?

Head to head, the answer comes down to behavior.

Acorns wins on automation. Round-ups plus recurring deposits plus a pre-built portfolio means you can genuinely set it and forget it. It's the better micro-investing app for someone who will otherwise do nothing.

Stash wins on education and control. If you want to learn how markets work and have a say in what you own, Stash gives you the tools. It's also the better pick if you eventually want to buy specific funds or stocks.

Both charge monthly fees, and both are aimed at small, growing balances. If you're deciding between them and you're honest with yourself about whether you'll actually log in and learn — pick accordingly.

Robo-Advisor Comparison: When Betterment Makes More Sense

Once you have a few hundred dollars or more, or once you're investing specifically for retirement, Betterment's model starts to make more sense than a flat monthly subscription.

The percentage fee is predictable, the portfolio is professionally diversified, and rebalancing happens without you lifting a finger. If your goal is "build wealth over 20 years and don't think about it," that's the structure you want. If your goal is "save my spare change and learn along the way," Acorns or Stash will feel more natural.

They're not really competitors — they're different stages of the same journey.

How to Invest Small Amounts Without Wasting Them

A few principles that apply no matter which app you choose:

  • Automate a recurring deposit. Even $10 a week beats sporadic $200 deposits you never get around to.
  • Grab free money first. In the US, contribute enough to your employer's retirement plan to earn the full match before investing anywhere else.
  • Build a small cash buffer. Investing money you might need next month is how beginners get forced to sell at the worst time.
  • Keep costs proportional. A $3 monthly fee on a $200 balance is a big drag. Use percentage-fee options or wait until your balance is larger.
  • Choose diversified funds over individual stocks while you're learning.
  • Don't check the app daily. Volatility is normal. Staring at it makes you trade, and trading makes you poorer.
  • Increase contributions when your income rises. Automate that too.

Investing involves risk, including the loss of principal. Nothing here is personalized financial advice.

FAQ

What's the best investing app for beginners with $10?

Any of the three can work with a small amount, but the deciding factor is fees. A flat monthly subscription eats a large percentage of a $10 balance, so start with a recurring deposit plan and expect the first year to be about building the habit, not the balance. Check each app's current minimums and fee schedule before committing.

Is Acorns or Stash better for a first-time investor?

Acorns is better if you want automation and minimal decisions. Stash is better if you want to learn, choose some of your own investments, and gradually take more control. There's no universal winner — it depends on whether you'll actually engage with the app or need it to run itself.

Can you lose money with a robo-advisor?

Yes. Robo-advisors invest in markets, and markets go down. Diversification and automatic rebalancing reduce risk, but they don't eliminate it. These accounts are not FDIC insured, and you should only invest money you won't need in the short term.

The Bottom Line

For a beginner in the US or EU starting with small amounts, the smartest move is to pick the app that matches your personality. Choose Acorns if you want saving to happen without thinking. Choose Stash if you want to learn and have a say. Choose Betterment if you have a clear long-term goal, a growing balance, and no interest in picking investments yourself.

None of them requires you to be rich to start. They just require you to start.
