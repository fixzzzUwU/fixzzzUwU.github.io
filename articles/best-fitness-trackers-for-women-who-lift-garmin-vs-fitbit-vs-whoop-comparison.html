Best Fitness Trackers for Women Who Lift: Garmin vs Fitbit vs Whoop Comparison

If you spend your gym time under a barbell rather than on a treadmill, you've probably noticed that most fitness trackers weren't designed with you in mind. They count steps, nag you about cardio zones, and call a 45-minute squat session "1,200 steps." Meanwhile, the numbers that actually matter — working sets, load progression, rest intervals, recovery — get buried or ignored entirely.

The good news: the best fitness tracker for weightlifting has gotten genuinely better. Garmin, Fitbit, Whoop, and Apple Watch all now offer some form of strength training mode, but they approach it very differently. This comparison breaks down what each brand does well for women who lift, so you can pick the one that fits your training style instead of the one with the flashiest marketing.

Why Most Fitness Trackers Struggle With Strength Training

Wrist-based trackers rely primarily on optical heart rate sensors and accelerometers. That works beautifully for running, cycling, and rowing, where the motion is repetitive and the heart rate climbs steadily. Lifting is the opposite: your heart rate spikes during a heavy set, drops during rest, and your wrist barely moves during a slow, controlled rep.

That mismatch is why a smartwatch for strength training can show a "calorie burn" that feels wildly off, or log a deadlift session as a leisurely stroll. It's not a flaw in your device — it's a limitation of the technology. The brands that handle lifting best are the ones that let you manually log sets, reps, and weight, then layer heart rate and recovery data on top.

What to Look For in a Fitness Tracker for Lifting

Before comparing brands, here's the short checklist that actually matters for strength athletes.

Strength-specific logging

You want a device that lets you build a workout on the fly — add an exercise, log weight and reps, and rest between sets. Bonus points if it syncs to a platform like Garmin Connect, Fitbit's app, or Strava so your history lives in one place.

Rest timers and set tracking

A built-in rest timer sounds trivial until you're mid-workout and don't want to unlock your phone with chalky hands. Auto set detection is improving, but manual logging is still more accurate for compound lifts.

Recovery and readiness scores

This is where Whoop built its reputation and where Garmin and Fitbit have caught up. A readiness or body battery score can help you decide whether to push for a PR or take a deload — especially useful if you're training four or more days a week.

Comfort and fit

Women's wrists are, on average, smaller than men's, and a bulky watch can interfere with wrist straps, lifting hooks, or a barbell knurling against your forearm. Look for a 40–43mm case or a band-style tracker if you're doing a lot of Olympic lifting.

Battery life and app quality

If you're charging nightly, you'll eventually stop wearing it. And a tracker is only as good as the app you review your data in.

Garmin vs Fitbit vs Whoop: How They Compare

Garmin — Best Overall for Serious Lifters

Garmin has quietly become the strongest option for women who strength train. Its strength training activity profile lets you log sets and reps directly on the watch, and the Garmin Connect app shows a clean breakdown of volume, personal records, and muscle group focus over time. You also get Body Battery, training readiness, and sleep tracking in one ecosystem.

The trade-off is complexity. Garmin's menus are deep, and the entry-level models have smaller, dimmer displays than an Apple Watch. But for a fitness tracker for lifting that doubles as a running and hiking watch, it's hard to beat.

Best for: lifters who want real workout logging, long battery life, and multi-sport versatility.

Fitbit — Best for Everyday Wellness and Budget

Fitbit's strength training mode is simpler: you start a "weights" workout, and the device tracks heart rate and duration while you log details in the app afterward. You won't get on-wrist set tracking like Garmin, but you do get excellent sleep scores, stress management tools, and a friendly app that's easy to stick with.

Fitbit is also the most comfortable for all-day wear and the most affordable entry point. If your priority is building healthy habits around training rather than obsessing over volume charts, this is a sensible pick.

Best for: beginners, casual lifters, and anyone who wants wellness tracking first and gym metrics second.

Whoop — Best for Recovery-Focused Athletes

Whoop takes a different approach: no screen, no notifications, just a band that measures strain, recovery, and sleep. For heavy lifters, the recovery score is the headline feature — it factors in heart rate variability, resting heart rate, and sleep to tell you how hard you should train today.

The catch is the subscription model. Whoop hardware is bundled with a monthly membership, which can add up over years. And because there's no display, you'll still need your phone or a separate timer for logging sets.

Best for: data-driven athletes who train hard, sleep seriously, and want guidance on when to back off.

Apple Watch — Best for iPhone Users

Apple Watch has improved its strength training support considerably, with custom workouts, rest timers, and third-party apps like Strong and Hevy that turn it into a genuine lifting companion. If you're already in the Apple ecosystem, the integration is seamless.

The downside is battery life — you'll likely charge it daily — and the fact that the best lifting experience often depends on a third-party app rather than the native one.

How to Get Accurate Data From Any Tracker

Whichever device you choose, a few habits make a real difference:

  • Log your sets manually. Auto-detection is improving but still misses reps on slow eccentrics and machine work.
  • Wear the band snug — but not tight. A loose fit lets light in and throws off optical heart rate readings.
  • Position it above the wrist bone. This is the single biggest fix for inaccurate readings during lifting.
  • Use a chest strap for heavy compound days. If precision matters, a Bluetooth chest strap paired to your watch is far more reliable than optical sensors.
  • Review weekly, not daily. Volume trends and recovery patterns over four weeks tell you far more than any single session.

FAQ

Is Garmin or Fitbit better for weightlifting?

Garmin is generally better for weightlifting because it supports on-watch set and rep logging, plus training readiness metrics. Fitbit is easier to live with and cheaper, but you'll do most of your strength logging in the app afterward rather than on your wrist.

Do fitness trackers accurately count calories during strength training?

Not precisely. Wrist-based sensors struggle with the stop-start nature of lifting, so calorie estimates tend to be rough. Treat them as a general trend rather than a precise number, and focus on consistency and progressive overload instead.

Can I wear a fitness tracker while lifting heavy?

Yes, but placement matters. Wear it above the wrist bone and consider a band-style tracker or a smaller case if you use wrist straps, hooks, or do a lot of Olympic lifting, where a bulky watch can get in the way.

Final Thoughts

There's no single best fitness watch in 2025 for every lifter — it depends on whether you want detailed workout logging, recovery guidance, or simple habit tracking. If you train seriously and want your watch to keep up, Garmin is the most complete package. If recovery is your priority, Whoop earns its subscription. If you want something comfortable, affordable, and easy to stick with, Fitbit is a solid starting point.

Pick the one that matches how you actually train, not the one with the longest feature list. The tracker that stays on your wrist is the one that works.
